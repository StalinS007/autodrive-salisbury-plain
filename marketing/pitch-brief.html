The $2,500 Case

What this is: the market research, the value maths, and the exact way to anchor a $2,500-a-month retainer with Jitty tomorrow — with a walk-away line you can actually hold. Sections tagged show Jitty can go straight into his proposal; sections tagged your eyes only are your playbook.

The short version

  1. $2,500 is not expensive — it's under market for the scope. Bought in pieces at Australian freelance rates, what you're proposing costs $5,200–9,600 a month. Auto-specific agencies (US) sit at $1,500–6,000/mo before video. You are selling a ~50% discount, not a premium.
  2. Break-even is one used car a month. Or eleven Standard services. Or one panel job plus six services. Say it in cars, not dollars — that's the unit Jitty thinks in.
  3. Anchor from the top. Show a $3,800 option first, then $2,500 as "the one that fits you", then a deliberately thin $1,500 that is obviously a downgrade. He should feel he's choosing the middle, not being sold the top.
  4. Lead with what he's already received. The website, the booking flow, the tracking, the playbook, the calendar tool — roughly $8–12k of work at market rates, delivered on trust. The trial has already run. You're not asking him to gamble; you're asking him to stop getting it free.
  5. The walk-away is a scope line, not a threat. Below $2,500 you cannot run a system — only posts. And posts are exactly what he has now. Say that plainly, once, and mean it.

What happens when a workshop takes this seriously

Royalty Auto Service, St Marys, Georgia. A father-and-son independent shop. Two years of consistent short-form video — not polished, just real repairs explained to camera. Result: customers now drive from Alabama, the Carolinas and Delaware, some staying overnight in a hotel, to get their car fixed at "the shop from YouTube". A third location is opening. Their own advice from the trade press: simpler topics perform best (their breakout video was a window reset), and authenticity beats polish every time. That's Jitty's exact advantage — he's authentic on camera already.

Australia. The honest picture: no Australian workshop has gone that big on video yet — which is the opportunity, not the caveat. What the local data does show is that the decision is made online: car owners Google first, and the workshop in the top three map results with the best reviews gets the booking. AutoGuru's national "Top Mechanics" list is built on review volume. A Brisbane workshop with only 260 Instagram followers has held a top-5 AutoGuru rating three years running — the lesson being that local trust signals (reviews, profile, consistency) matter more than follower counts. Jitty has the 5.0★ rating; the system's job is to point every video at it.

Terms that protect you

  • 6-month minimum, price locked. Content compounds; three months proves nothing and he'll judge it at month two. Say "six months, we review at three" — the review makes the six feel safe.
  • Paid on the 1st, in advance. Not "end of month". You're funding shoots and tools up front.
  • Ad spend, print, and paid tools are his. Your fee is your work. Never absorb Meta's bill.
  • Add-ons instead of discounts. If he wants more than Grow but baulks at Dominate: in-language pack +$450, extra shoot day +$450, boosting management +$400 or 15% of spend. The $2,500 base never moves; the "more" you want comes from here.
  • What you'll never do: guarantee virality, guarantee a booking count, or work without the two-week buffer. Say the first two out loud — refusing to promise is what makes the rest believable.
The one number you don't know: his revenue. Australian small businesses spend 7–10% of revenue on marketing; $2,500 a month sits at 7–10% of $25–36k a month ($300–430k a year). If he does that, this is a normal budget. If he does much less — say under $20k a month — $2,500 is over 12% and will feel like a bite, and you'll need the used-car margin argument to do the heavy lifting. Try to find out before you quote, even roughly: "How many cars a week do you put through?" × his average ticket gets you close enough.
How to anchor it your eyes only

The order of the conversation

Value-based selling means the price arrives last, after he's already agreed on what it's worth. Don't let the number come up early. If he asks, say "I'll get to that — I want to show you what you're paying for first," and keep going.

  1. Open with what he's already got. Walk the "already delivered" list, out loud, in his shop, on his phone. Show him the booking flow working. End with the low number. "That's about eight thousand dollars of work, done on a handshake. I did it because I believed the business deserved it. Now I want to show you what happens when it's a system instead of a favour."
  2. Show his own numbers. The 44%, the 29.9K, the spike days, Cyril's booking. Not slides — the actual analytics screen. "Your reels are already 44% of your website traffic. You did that without trying. Imagine five a week, every week, all ending at the same door."
  3. Name the failure mode without blaming him. "You had one go viral. What did we get from it? Nothing — because nothing was ready to catch it. That's not your fault; it's what happens without a system."
  4. Ask the break-even question and wait. "If this brings you one extra used-car buyer a month — just one — does it pay for itself?" Then say nothing until he answers.
  5. Give the market number before yours. "If you rang an agency for this — filming, editing, five posts a week, the groups, Google, the website — the cheapest quote you'd get is about five thousand a month."
  6. Present three options, top first. Slide the sheet across. Describe Dominate in two sentences, then Grow in four, then Maintain in one — dismissively. "Dominate is where we'd go if you wanted to own the whole area. Grow is the one I've built for you — it's the system, everything we talked about. Maintain is just me editing what you film — it's what you have now with a nicer finish."
  7. Say the price once, then stop talking. "Grow is two and a half thousand a month, six months, we review at three." Silence. The first person to speak loses. Let him.

When he pushes back

"That's a lot of money."
Agree, then reframe the unit. "It is. It's also eighty-two dollars a day — less than an hour on the hoist. And it's half what anyone else would charge for the same thing. What I'm asking is whether one extra car a month is worth eighty-two dollars a day."
"I get my customers from the Facebook groups anyway."
Don't argue — absorb it. "You do, and the groups are in the plan — every reel goes into them, with a link that shows us which group sends people. But the groups are rented. One admin change and you're gone. Everything I'm building — the reviews, the followers, the website — is yours."
"Can't I just keep doing it myself?"
Price his time. "You can. It's ten hours a week done properly, and your hour is worth more on a car than on a phone. Ten hours of your labour a week is six thousand dollars a month. I'm cheaper than you are."
"Can we do three months and see?"
Hold six, give the review. "Three months is exactly long enough to decide it isn't working before it's had a chance to. Six months, and at three we sit down with the numbers — if they're not moving, I'll tell you first."
"Can you do it for two thousand?"
Trade scope, never price. "I can do two thousand — that's Maintain plus one shoot a month. What comes out is the weekly used-car video and the groups system. Which of those do you want to lose?" He won't want to lose either. That's the point.

The walk-away line: “Below this it's not a system, it's posts — and posts are what you've got now. I'd rather not do it than do it in a way that lets you say social media doesn't work.”

Say it calmly, once, and mean it. This isn't a threat — it's the most credible sentence in the meeting, because it's the only one where you're arguing against your own interest. If he still says no, thank him, leave the sheet, and tell him the offer stands for a fortnight. Then actually stop the unpaid work.

Don'ts

  • Don't say "I think" or "maybe" anywhere near the price. "It's two and a half thousand." Full stop.
  • Don't apologise for the number, and don't fill the silence after it.
  • Don't discount. Every dollar you drop without removing scope tells him the first number was padding.
  • Don't promise virality or a booking count. Promise the system, the cadence, and the report.
  • Don't pitch in the workshop with cars waiting. Ask for 30 minutes sitting down — a chai, a phone, the sheet.
Honest caveats your eyes only

What could go wrong, and what to do about it

  • Five posts a week is a real load for one person. The two-week buffer isn't a nice-to-have — it's what stops one sick week from breaking the promise. Build it before you need it.
  • Jitty's two videos a week are the weak link. Give him the ten scripts, a phone tripod, and a Sunday reminder. If he doesn't film, don't let the cadence drop — you fill the gap with photo posts and the inventory video, and you say so in the report.
  • Month two is when he'll doubt it. Followers move first, leads move later. Your fortnightly report should show leading indicators (views, link taps, profile actions) so the lag doesn't look like failure.
  • The multilingual content needs a native speaker. If that's not you for Hindi or Punjabi, price a translator into the Dominate tier and keep Malayalam in Grow.
  • The ROI benchmarks above are from measured content programs, mostly B2B and SEO-led. Use them for the shape of the argument (old content keeps working), not as a promise of 300%.
